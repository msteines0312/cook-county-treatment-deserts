# Treatment Isn't Keeping Up Where Overdose Deaths Are Highest

**Cook County overdose deaths and access to opioid use disorder treatment, 2015-2025**
*Draft policy brief*

## The problem

Between 2015 and 2025, 15,343 people died of accidental drug overdoses in Cook County (deaths placed in a census tract). Deaths peaked at 2,060 in 2022 and have fallen since, but 913 people still died in 2025. Methadone and buprenorphine are the most effective treatments for opioid use disorder and cut the risk of overdose death sharply. The question is whether they're available where people are dying.

## What we found

**1. Clinics are close to the hardest-hit neighborhoods, but they're stretched thin.** Tracts with the highest overdose death rates are a median of 0.6 miles from a treatment site. That's closer than anywhere else in the county, so a distance map would call them well served. But measured against the number of people dying nearby, these same tracts have the least treatment supply in the county.

**2. A small share of the county carries a large share of deaths with the least treatment per death.** 227 census tracts, home to 12.4% of Cook County residents, account for 39.3% of overdose deaths. They have 4.2 treatment sites in reach per 100 annual overdose deaths, less than half the 9.8 in other high-burden tracts.

**3. These are neighborhoods where getting to a clinic is hardest.** In the underserved high-burden tracts, 30% of households don't have a car (21% in better-served high-burden tracts), 26% of residents live in poverty, and median household income is $43,951. The median tract is 80% Black. Most are on Chicago's West and South Sides.

**4. Car access tracks overdose rates more closely than any other factor we measured.** Across all tracts, each 10-point increase in households without a vehicle goes with a 20% higher overdose death rate, after accounting for poverty, insurance, and racial composition.

## What this suggests

- **Expand capacity where need is concentrated, not just coverage.** Opening clinics in new areas won't help the neighborhoods that already have one nearby but too much need for it. More slots, longer hours, and same-day starts at existing West and South Side sites address the gap this analysis finds.
- **Bring treatment to people who can't easily travel.** With about a third of households car-free, mobile methadone and buprenorphine units, telehealth buprenorphine starts, and transit passes tied to treatment enrollment remove the barrier most closely linked to overdose rates.
- **Make sure new capacity takes Medicaid.** Counting only sites that accept Medicaid, underserved tracts drop to 3.7 sites per 100 annual deaths.
- **Publish capacity data.** The public treatment directory lists locations but not how many patients a site can take or how long the wait is. Without that, no one can measure the real gap.

## How this was measured

Overdose deaths come from the Cook County Medical Examiner Case Archive. Treatment sites come from SAMHSA's FindTreatment.gov (sites that prescribe buprenorphine or are certified to dispense methadone). Demographics come from the Census Bureau's 2019-2023 American Community Survey. Treatment access uses a two-step floating catchment area method: for each neighborhood, the number of treatment sites within 2 miles, divided by the overdose deaths competing for them. Full methods and limitations are in [methodology.md](methodology.md).

## Limitations

The treatment directory doesn't report capacity, so every site counts the same regardless of size, and individual doctors who prescribe buprenorphine aren't listed. Deaths are counted where they happened, not where people lived. Access is measured by straight-line distance; transit travel time is the next step. Small numbers are suppressed to protect privacy. These findings describe neighborhood patterns and don't say anything about individuals.
